# Phase 2.2 Final Report: Electron–Sample Interactions in the SEM

**SEMICON 2026 Research Repository**
**Applied Materials**
**Date:** 2026-07-30
**Classification:** Research-Only (Phase 2.2)

---

## Executive Summary

Phase 2.2 answers the question: **"What physically happens when the focused electron beam strikes a semiconductor wafer?"** We have characterized every major interaction mechanism and classified each for its relevance to SEM simulation.

### Key Results

1. **The interaction volume** — the 3D region where primary electrons deposit energy — varies in size by ~25× between low-Z (photoresist) and high-Z (tungsten) materials at CD-SEM energies.

2. **Three signals matter** for semiconductor inspection simulation: SE-I (primary information carrier), SE-II (resolution-degrading background), and BSE (compositional contrast). All other signals (Auger, X-rays, CL, etc.) can be safely ignored.

3. **SEs dominate CD-SEM** because of their extreme surface sensitivity (escape depth 1–5 nm), strong topographic contrast ($\sec\theta$ angular dependence), high yield ($\delta_{\text{max}} > 1$), and compatibility with low-voltage operation.

4. **BSEs are essential for composition and voltage contrast** — their monotonic Z-dependence enables material discrimination, and their sensitivity to local electrical potential enables contact/via open detection.

5. **Seven core mechanisms** are identified as essential for any physically realistic SEM simulator, providing a clear engineering specification for subsequent phases.

## 1. Phase Results by Document

### 1.1 Electron–Sample Interaction (Document 02)

- Elastic scattering (Rutherford/Mott) redirects primary electrons; inelastic scattering (Bethe) deposits energy.
- The interaction volume shape transitions from teardrop (low-Z) to hemispherical (high-Z).
- Interaction depth scales approximately as $E_0^{1.7}$ and inversely with density and Z.
- SE-I are generated along the primary trajectory; SE-II are generated by exiting BSEs.
- Signal origins are layered: SE → top <5 nm, Auger → 0.5–3 nm, BSE → 0.1–1 μm, X-rays → 0.1–5 μm.

### 1.2 Secondary Electron Physics (Document 03)

- SE have energies <50 eV, peaking at 2–5 eV (Chung-Everhart distribution).
- Escape depth (IMFP) follows the universal curve: <1 nm for metals, 5–20 nm for insulators.
- SE yield $\delta$ peaks at 100–800 eV, with $\delta_{\text{max}}$ ranging from ~1.0 (W) to ~2.5 (SiO₂, resist).
- $\delta$ follows a $\sec\theta$ angular dependence — the physical origin of topographic contrast.
- The SE-I/SE-II classification is critical: SE-I provides the high-resolution signal; SE-II degrades resolution.

### 1.3 Backscattered Electron Physics (Document 04)

- BSE yield $\eta$ increases monotonically with Z (from ~0.06 for C to ~0.52 for W).
- $\eta$ at low energies (<5 keV) is higher than at high energies (the Joy model).
- BSE escape depth ~0.3× the Bethe range.
- BSE angular distribution is approximately cosine for flat surfaces.
- BSE voltage contrast enables electrical defect detection without probe contact.

### 1.4 Other Signals (Document 05)

| Signal | Relevant for Simulation? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Auger electrons | No | Signal too weak for grayscale imaging |
| Characteristic X-rays | No | Only relevant for EDS; far too coarse resolution |
| Bremsstrahlung | No | Only EDS background |
| Cathodoluminescence | No | Negligible for Si and most semiconductor materials |
| Specimen current | No | Lower resolution; complementary to SE/BSE |
| Transmitted electrons | No | Wafers are thick (not electron-transparent) |

### 1.5 Material Interactions (Document 06)

Material-specific behavior at CD-SEM energies (500 eV–1.5 keV) was characterized for six key semiconductor materials. Key findings:

- **SE yield varies by ~3×** across materials (W: ~0.7, resist: ~2.2 at 500 eV).
- **BSE yield varies by ~5×** across materials (resist: ~0.10, W: ~0.52 at 500 eV).
- **Escape depth varies by ~40×** (W: ~0.5 nm, resist: ~20 nm for SE).
- **Interaction depth varies by ~25×** (W: ~2 nm, resist: ~50 nm at 500 eV).
- The charge-neutral condition ($\sigma = 1$) occurs at different energies for different materials — this is critical for charging mitigation in CD-SEM.

### 1.6 Engineering Conclusions (Document 07)

All mechanisms were classified into three tiers:

| Tier | Count | Includes |
|---|---|---|
| **Essential** | 7 | Elastic scattering, inelastic scattering, SE-I, SE-II, BSE, material library, geometry-dependent yield |
| **Useful** | 7 | Plasmon losses, low-energy BSE model, crystallographic channeling, etc. |
| **Can be ignored** | 9 | Auger, X-rays, CL, specimen current, transmitted electrons, SE-III, etc. |

The recommended simulation approach is a **hybrid model**: Monte Carlo precomputation for ground-truth reference combined with analytical yield functions for the real-time renderer.

## 2. Critical Unresolved Questions

The following uncertainties were identified and should be addressed through targeted sensitivity analysis:

1. **SE-I / SE-II ratio** — Published values range from 30:70 to 70:30. The exact ratio depends on material and energy.
2. **Thin-film BSE yield at <1 keV** — Empirical formulas were developed for bulk materials; thin-film corrections are less established.
3. **SE IMFP for insulators at very low energies (<200 eV)** — Less well-characterized than for metals.
4. **$\sec\theta$ validity for extreme topographies** — The standard angular dependence model may be inaccurate for vertical sidewalls and re-entrant profiles.
5. **Bethe stopping power for photoresists** — The composition of resists varies; the mean ionization potential $J$ is not precisely known.

## 3. Knowledge Required for Phase 2.3

Phase 2.2 has established **how signals are generated** when the beam strikes the sample. Phase 2.3 must answer:

**How do these generated signals become the grayscale SEM image?**

The following questions must be addressed:

### Contrast Formation
1. How does the SE/BSE yield at each sample point translate into spatial contrast across a patterned wafer?
2. What is edge brightening and what is its physical origin in terms of SE generation?
3. How do different materials produce different grayscale levels in SE and BSE images?
4. How does surface topography modulate the detected signal at sloped surfaces, shadowed regions, and sidewalls?
5. What are the specific contrast mechanisms in CD-SEM that enable precise edge detection?

### Signal Collection and Detection
6. How does the detector collection efficiency vary with position on the sample (detector geometry effects)?
7. How does the detector transfer function map emitted electrons to pixel intensity?
8. How does the TTL SE detector selectively collect SE-I vs. SE-II through the objective lens field?

### Pixel Formation and Image Properties
9. How is the pixel grayscale value determined from the number of detected electrons?
10. How does the finite probe size (beam diameter) convolve with the sample's yield distribution?
11. What is the role of pixel sampling density in measuring sub-pixel feature positions?
12. What does an SEM image of a simple line/space pattern look like, and how does the line profile (intensity vs. position) form from the underlying physics?
13. How do SE and BSE images of the same structure differ in appearance and information content?

### Scope of Phase 2.3

Phase 2.3 should produce a complete understanding of:
- The physical origin of contrast in SE and BSE images
- The detector model linking emitted electrons to pixel values
- The probe-sample convolution that determines the edge profile
- The basic line/space image and its relationship to the underlying structure
- **Everything between "the signals leave the sample" and "the grayscale image appears"**

This knowledge completes the bridge between signal generation (Phase 2.2) and the full understanding of how an SEM image of a semiconductor pattern is formed.
