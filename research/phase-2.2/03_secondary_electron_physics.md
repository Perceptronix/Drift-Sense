## 1. Introduction

Secondary electrons (SEs) are the most important signal in semiconductor SEM inspection. They dominate CD metrology, defect review, and high-resolution imaging because they provide the **highest spatial resolution** of any SEM signal. Understanding SE physics in detail is essential for any subsequent modeling or simulation of SEM images.

**Definition:** Secondary electrons are electrons emitted from a sample with kinetic energies less than 50 eV, generated through inelastic scattering of primary or backscattered electrons within the solid.

---

## 2. Generation Mechanism

### 2.1 Inelastic Energy Transfer

SEs are produced when a primary electron (PE) or backscattered electron collides with an atomic electron and transfers a portion of its kinetic energy sufficient to overcome the material's binding energy (work function for metals, electron affinity for insulators). The process can be represented as:

$$\text{PE} (E_0) + \text{Atom} \rightarrow \text{PE} (E_0 - \Delta E) + \text{Atom}^+ + e^-_{\text{secondary}}$$

where $\Delta E$ must exceed the material's ionization potential or work function.

### 2.2 Energy Distribution

**Fact:** The energy distribution of secondary electrons follows a characteristic shape — a sharp peak at very low energies (1–5 eV) with a long tail extending up to 50 eV. The distribution peaks at approximately 2–5 eV for most materials.

The empirical formula for the SE energy distribution (after Chung and Everhart, 1974) is:

$$\frac{dN}{dE} \propto \frac{E}{(E + \Phi)^4}$$

where $\Phi$ is the work function (metals) or electron affinity (insulators/semiconductors).

**Inference:** The vast majority of SEs have energies below 10 eV. This is critical because:
1. Low-energy electrons have very short inelastic mean free paths → they can only escape from near the surface.
2. The detector bias (typically +200–300 V) is sufficient to collect nearly all SEs regardless of their initial direction.

### 2.3 SE-I, SE-II, and SE-III Classification

A critical distinction for understanding SEM resolution:

| Type | Generated By | Generation Location | Contribution to Image |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SE‑I** | Primary electron (entering) | At or near the beam impact point (~0–2 nm laterally) | Highest resolution (limited by probe size) |
| **SE‑II** | Backscattered electrons (exiting) | Up to ~1 μm from beam impact point | Lower resolution background signal |
| **SE‑III** | Backscattered electrons striking chamber walls or polepiece | Far from the specimen | Uniform background, reduces contrast |

**Fact:** SE-I carry the high-resolution information essential for CD metrology. SE-II degrade resolution by adding signal from regions far from the beam impact. SE-III are pure background and reduce image contrast.

**Recommendation for simulation:** The SE-I/SE-II/SE-III distinction must be preserved in any physically accurate SEM simulation. SE-I should be modeled as the primary information carrier. SE-II should be treated as a resolution-degrading additive background. SE-III can be ignored or modeled as a constant offset.

## 3. Escape Depth

### 3.1 Inelastic Mean Free Path (IMFP)

The distance an SE can travel within the solid before losing energy through further inelastic scattering is governed by the **inelastic mean free path** (IMFP), which depends on the electron energy and the material.

**Fact:** The IMFP for electrons in the 1–50 eV range follows the "universal curve" — a U-shaped function with a minimum of approximately 0.5–2 nm at 50–100 eV and rising at both lower and higher energies.

For energies below 10 eV (where most SEs are found):

| Material | Approximate IMFP at 5 eV | Approximate IMFP at 20 eV |
|---|---|---|
| Metals (Cu, W) | ~0.5–1 nm | ~1–2 nm |
| Semiconductors (Si) | ~1–3 nm | ~2–5 nm |
| Insulators (SiO₂) | ~5–20 nm | ~10–30 nm |

**Inference:** For metals, only SEs generated within the top ~1 nm can escape. For insulators, the escape depth is larger (up to 20 nm for SiO₂). This means:
- Metals produce SE images with the highest resolution but lower yield.
- Insulators can produce higher SE yield but with somewhat lower resolution due to the larger escape depth.

### 3.2 Escape Probability

The probability that an SE generated at depth $z$ reaches the surface and escapes is exponentially decaying:

$$P(z) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{z}{\lambda_e \cos\theta}\right)$$

where $\lambda_e$ is the effective escape depth (typically 0.5–5 nm) and $\theta$ is the angle relative to the surface normal.

**Fact:** Approximately 63% of detected SEs originate from within one escape depth of the surface. For a typical metal, ~95% of SEs come from the top 3 nm.

## 4. Secondary Electron Yield

### 4.1 Definition

The secondary electron yield $\delta$ is defined as the number of SEs emitted per incident primary electron:

$$\delta = \frac{I_{SE}}{I_P}$$

where $I_{SE}$ is the SE current and $I_P$ is the primary beam current.

**Fact:** Unlike BSE yield, SE yield depends on many factors: material, beam energy, surface condition, surface contamination, crystallography, and incident angle.

### 4.2 Dependence on Beam Energy

The SE yield as a function of primary beam energy follows a **universal shape**:

1. Increases from zero at very low energies (below the emission threshold).
2. Reaches a maximum $\delta_{\text{max}}$ at an energy $E_{\text{max}}$ (typically 100–800 eV).
3. Decreases at higher energies because the primary electrons penetrate deeper and SEs generated deeper cannot escape.

**Representative values:**

| Material | $\delta_{\text{max}}$ | $E_{\text{max}}$ (eV) |
|---|---|---|
| Si | ~1.1 | ~250 |
| SiO₂ | ~2.5 | ~400 |
| Cu | ~1.3 | ~600 |
| W | ~1.0 | ~700 |
| Photoresist (organic) | ~2.0–3.0 | ~200–300 |

**Inference:** Most materials have $\delta_{\text{max}} > 1$, meaning more SEs are emitted than primary electrons incident. At higher energies (5–30 keV), $\delta$ typically falls to 0.1–0.5.

### 4.3 Dependence on Incident Angle

The SE yield increases with the angle of incidence $\theta$ (measured from the surface normal) approximately as:

$$\delta(\theta) = \delta(0) \cdot \frac{1}{\cos\theta}$$

**Fact:** This $\sec\theta$ dependence is the physical origin of **topographic contrast** in SE images. Sloped surfaces and edges produce more SEs than flat surfaces. At a vertical step edge, the effective yield increase can reach 2–4× the flat-surface value.

**Inference:** The strong angular dependence of SE yield is what makes SE imaging so effective for revealing surface topography. In CD-SEM, the enhanced yield at pattern edges provides the signal edge profile used for dimension measurement.

### 4.4 Dependence on Material

For flat surfaces at normal incidence and fixed beam energy:

| Material | SE Yield $\delta$ (at 1 keV) | SE Yield $\delta$ (at 10 keV) |
|---|---|---|
| Si | ~1.0 | ~0.15 |
| SiO₂ | ~1.8 | ~0.3 |
| Si₃N₄ | ~1.5 | ~0.25 |
| Cu | ~1.1 | ~0.2 |
| W | ~0.8 | ~0.1 |
| Photoresist | ~2.0 | ~0.4 |

## 5. Dependence on Accelerating Voltage

### 5.1 Yield Curve Shape

The SE yield vs. energy curve has three characteristic regions:

| Region | Energy Range | Behavior | SEM Relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rising** | 0 to $E_{\text{max}}$ | $\delta$ increases with $E$ | Below ~300 eV: surface-sensitive, low damage |
| **Peak** | $E_{\text{max}}$ | $\delta = \delta_{\text{max}}$ | Optimal SNR for surface imaging |
| **Falling** | $> E_{\text{max}}$ | $\delta$ decreases as $1/E$ or slower | Higher energy: deeper penetration, lower surface sensitivity |

### 5.2 Charge Neutrality Condition

The **total electron yield** is:

$$\sigma = \delta + \eta$$

where $\delta$ is SE yield and $\eta$ is BSE yield.

When $\sigma = 1$, the sample emits exactly as many electrons as it receives — the **charge-neutral condition**. This occurs at two beam energies:
- **$E_1$:** Low energy (typically 50–200 eV), where $\delta$ is rising.
- **$E_2$:** High energy (typically 1–5 keV), where $\delta$ is falling.

**Fact:** For semiconductor inspection, operating near the charge-neutral condition ($\sigma \approx 1$) is desirable to minimize charging artifacts. For SiO₂ on Si, this occurs at approximately 500–800 eV for typical structures.

### 5.3 SE Yield Below 1 keV

At beam energies below 1 keV (the regime of modern CD-SEM):
- The SE yield is higher because the primary electrons deposit their energy closer to the surface.
- More SEs can escape before being reabsorbed.
- The $\sec\theta$ angular dependence is even stronger.

## 6. Surface Sensitivity

### 6.1 Why SEs Are Surface-Sensitive

**Fact:** The extreme surface sensitivity of SE imaging arises from three physical factors:

1. **Short IMFP at low energies:** SEs with 1–10 eV energies have IMFPs of 0.5–5 nm in most solids. They cannot escape from depths greater than ~5 nm regardless of the primary beam energy.

2. **Energy deposition profile:** At any primary beam energy, the energy deposited within the SE escape depth ($\sim$1–5 nm) determines the SE yield. Low energies deposit more energy near the surface.

3. **Production near the surface:** SEs produced by the primary beam (SE-I) are generated along the entire trajectory, but only those within the escape depth contribute. SEs produced by backscattered electrons on their way out of the sample (SE-II) extend the information depth slightly.

### 6.2 Information Depth

| Primary Energy | Information Depth (Si) | What Is Sampled |
|---|---|---|
| 500 eV | ~1–2 nm | Native oxide, top surface layers |
| 1 keV | ~2–5 nm | Near-surface features |
| 5 keV | ~5–10 nm | Includes some subsurface structure |
| 20 keV | ~10–20 nm | Mix of surface and subsurface |

## 7. Angular Dependence

### 7.1 Effect on SE Trajectories

SEs are emitted from the surface with a **cosine angular distribution**:

$$\frac{dN}{d\Omega} \propto \cos\phi$$

where $\phi$ is the angle from the surface normal. This distribution holds for flat surfaces when the primary beam is at normal incidence.

### 7.2 Effect on SE Collection

The angular distribution affects the collection efficiency of different detector configurations:
- **Axial (TTL) detectors:** Collect SEs emitted at small $\phi$ (near-normal).
- **Side-mounted (E-T) detectors:** Collect SEs preferentially from one side, producing shadowing.

## 8. Why Secondary Electrons Dominate Semiconductor SEM

### 8.1 Five Decisive Advantages

| Advantage | Physical Origin | Consequence for Semiconductor Inspection |
|---|---|---|
| **Highest resolution** | SE-I localized within probe diameter; short escape depth limits signal spread | Can resolve sub-5 nm features, measure CD |
| **Surface sensitivity** | Escape depth of 1–5 nm | Only the patterned top surface is imaged; bulk structure does not interfere |
| **Strong topographic contrast** | $\delta(\theta) \propto \sec\theta$ | Pattern edges and steps produce strong signal — enables edge detection |
| **High yield** | $\delta_{\text{max}} > 1$ for most materials | Good SNR even at low beam currents (10–20 pA) |
| **Low beam energy compatible** | Yield increases at low $E$ | Imaging at 300–800 eV minimizes beam damage and charging |

### 8.2 SE vs. BSE for Semiconductor Inspection

| Criterion | SE | BSE |
|---|---|---|
| Resolution | 0.5–5 nm (probe-limited) | 10–500 nm (interaction volume-limited) |
| Information | Topography dominant | Composition dominant |
| Yield at 1 keV | 0.8–2.0 | 0.05–0.50 |
| Surface sensitivity | High (1–5 nm) | Low (100–1000 nm) |
| Best for | CD measurement, defect review (topographic) | Material identification, voltage contrast |

**Inference:** For CD-SEM, SEs are the primary signal because they provide the required spatial resolution. BSEs serve a complementary role for material identification and voltage contrast inspection.
