# Phase 2.2 Executive Summary: Electron–Sample Interactions in SEM

**SEMICON 2026 Research Repository**
**Applied Materials**
**Date:** 2026-07-30
**Classification:** Research-Only (Phase 2.2)

---

## Purpose

Phase 2.1 established how the SEM instrument generates, focuses, and scans an electron beam. Phase 2.2 addresses the next critical question: **what physically happens when that focused electron beam strikes a semiconductor wafer?**

This phase characterizes every signal generated by the electron–sample interaction, establishes which signals matter for semiconductor inspection, and provides the physical foundation needed for all subsequent phases (contrast formation, noise modeling, image simulation).

---

## Key Findings

### 1. The Interaction Volume

When a primary electron beam enters a solid, it does not remain at the surface. Electrons penetrate and scatter within a **three-dimensional interaction volume** defined by:

- **Beam energy:** Higher voltage → deeper penetration (proportional to $E^{1.7}$ approximately).
- **Material density:** Higher density → smaller interaction volume.
- **Atomic number:** Higher Z → more forward scattering → less lateral spread.

The shape transitions from teardrop (low-Z materials like Si, C) to hemispherical (high-Z materials like W, Au) as atomic number increases. For silicon at 1 keV, the interaction depth is approximately 20–50 nm; at 20 keV, it extends to ~3–5 μm.

### 2. Signals Generated Within the Interaction Volume

| Signal | Energy Range | Information Depth | Key Information |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SE‑I** (primary SE) | 0–50 eV | 0–2 nm | Highest-resolution topography |
| **SE‑II** | 0–50 eV | 0–2 nm (generated deeper) | Lower-resolution topography (from BSE) |
| **BSE** | >50 eV (up to $E_0$) | 0.1–1 μm | Composition (Z-contrast) |
| **Auger electrons** | 50 eV–2 keV | 0.5–3 nm | Surface composition (elemental) |
| **Characteristic X-rays** | 0.1–20 keV | 0.1–5 μm | Elemental identification (EDS) |
| **Bremsstrahlung** | 0 – $E_0$ (continuous) | Bulk | Background in EDS |
| **Cathodoluminescence** | 1–5 eV (visible–UV) | Depth-dependent | Band structure, defects |
| **Specimen current** | Measured current | Full interaction volume | Absorbed electrons, complementary to SE |

### 3. Secondary Electron Physics

**Fact:** Secondary electrons are the dominant signal for high-resolution semiconductor inspection for three physical reasons:

1. **Low energy (peak <5 eV):** Their low energy means the inelastic mean free path in solids is extremely short — 0.5–2 nm in metals, 2–20 nm in insulators. Only SEs generated within ~1–5 nm of the surface can escape.
2. **Surface localization:** Because escape depth is far smaller than the interaction volume, SEs carry information from the immediate surface region — exactly where semiconductor structures are patterned.
3. **High yield:** SE yield ($\delta$) typically ranges from 0.1 to 2.0 depending on material and beam energy. For most materials at 1 keV, more SEs are emitted than BSEs.

**SE classification:**
- **SE‑I:** Generated at the beam impact point by the primary beam. These carry the highest-resolution information (resolution ≈ probe diameter).
- **SE‑II:** Generated by BSEs exiting the surface at distances up to ~1 μm from the beam impact. These degrade resolution.
- **SE‑III:** Generated by BSEs striking chamber walls or the polepiece. These add background signal.

### 4. Backscattered Electron Physics

**Fact:** BSE yield ($\eta$) increases monotonically with atomic number $Z$, following approximately:

$$\eta \approx -0.0254 + 0.016 Z - 0.000186 Z^2 + 8\times 10^{-7} Z^3$$

This Z-dependence makes BSE imaging the primary method for **compositional contrast** — materials with different Z appear with different brightness regardless of topography.

Key characteristics:
- BSE energy: >50 eV, most BSEs have >50% of the primary beam energy.
- BSE escape depth: approximately 0.3× the Bethe range (30–50% of interaction depth).
- BSE yield is nearly independent of beam energy above ~10 keV but increases at low energies (<5 keV).
- BSE angular distribution follows a cosine law for flat surfaces, peaked forward for tilted surfaces.

### 5. Semiconductor Material Effects

For the semiconductor materials most relevant to this project:

| Material | Formula | Avg Z | BSE Yield $\eta$ (20 keV) | SE Yield $\delta$ (max) | Interaction Depth (1 keV / 10 keV) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Silicon | Si | 14 | ~0.17 | ~1.1 | 30 nm / 3 μm |
| Silicon dioxide | SiO₂ | 10 (avg) | ~0.13 | ~1.5–2.5 | 50 nm / 4 μm |
| Silicon nitride | Si₃N₄ | ~11 | ~0.14 | ~1.8 | 40 nm / 3.5 μm |
| Copper | Cu | 29 | ~0.31 | ~1.3 | 15 nm / 1 μm |
| Tungsten | W | 74 | ~0.50 | ~1.0 | 5 nm / 0.4 μm |
| Photoresist (organic) | C-based | ~4–6 | ~0.06–0.08 | ~1.5–2.5 | 100 nm / 8 μm |

### 6. Engineering Classification

All interaction mechanisms were classified into three tiers:

| Tier | Description | Included Topics | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Essential** | Must be modeled for physically realistic SEM simulation | SE‑I generation, BSE yield, elastic scattering, energy loss, escape depth | Build into core physics engine |
| **Useful** | Improves accuracy but not required for first implementation | SE‑II/III, SE energy distribution, angular distributions | Add in later iterations |
| **Can be ignored** | Marginal impact on semiconductor inspection simulation | Auger, CL, X-rays, channeling, magnetic contrast | Document but do not implement |

## Phase 2.3 Knowledge Required

The following understanding is required to bridge from signal generation (Phase 2.2) to contrast formation and grayscale image generation (Phase 2.3):

1. How does the SE/BSE yield at each point on the sample translate into spatial contrast across a patterned wafer?
2. How does edge brightening (the SE signal enhancement at topographic edges) arise physically?
3. How do different materials produce different grayscale levels at a given beam energy?
4. What is the role of the detector transfer function in determining pixel intensity from the emitted signal?
5. How does the surface topography modulate the detected signal at shadowed and tilted surfaces?
6. What are the contrast mechanisms in CD-SEM that enable precise edge detection?
7. How does the emitted signal distribution relate to the final grayscale image?
